Jetting Versus Snaking: Which Tool Fits Your Clog

A drain snake, also called a cable or auger, bores a hole through a blockage. That restores flow fast, but the grease or root mass often stays stuck to the pipe wall, and the opening narrows again in weeks or months. Jetting washes the wall itself, so the pipe returns close to its full diameter.

Neither tool is better in every case. A single hair clog in a tub trap needs a cable, not a pressure hose. A line that has been snaked three times in one year needs a different answer.

Before anyone runs a jetter through your line, the state eLIPSE lookup on mass.gov lets you confirm the plumber's license, which is the standard check in Massachusetts.

Grease and Roots: The Two Jobs Jetting Does Best

Kitchen grease cools inside the pipe and builds up like cholesterol in an artery. A cable pokes a channel through it, then the rest slowly closes again. A rotating jet nozzle cuts the grease loose and carries it down the line.

Roots are different. Fine hair roots enter at joints looking for water and thicken into a mat that catches paper. Jetting with the right nozzle clears the mat, though it does nothing to the joint gap that let the roots in. That is why a line with a root problem usually needs a repeat visit schedule or a repair, and we say so up front.

If your trouble is mostly in one sink, start with drain cleaning. Jetting earns its place on the main line, where the pipe is long, wide and shared by every fixture.

Why the Camera Goes In Before the Nozzle

Cable and camera pass

We open a cleanout or pull a toilet, then send a camera down to see where the pipe is clear, coated, rooted or broken.

Decide on jetting

If the walls are intact, we choose a nozzle and pressure for the pipe material and the type of buildup.

Jet from the cleanout

The nozzle pulls itself downstream on its own jets, then we work it back up the line so debris travels toward the cleanout.

Camera again

A second pass confirms the pipe is clean and shows any damage the buildup was hiding.

Skipping the first pass is how people end up with water pouring into a collapsed section. The camera costs minutes and protects the pipe.

Pipes That Should Not Be Jetted

High-pressure water is not gentle. Cast iron that has rusted into thin scale, clay tile with cracked joints and fiber conduit pipe can fail under a jet stream. About 43.5 percent of the housing stock in town predates 1960, so old drain material is a real possibility on many streets.

When the camera shows a crushed, offset or missing section, we stop and talk about sewer line repair instead. You can also ask for a recorded look through a sewer inspection before you decide anything. If jetting is still safe, we may use lower pressure and a gentler nozzle, and we tell you why.

Gurgling in other fixtures

Flushing a toilet makes the tub or floor drain burp. Air is fighting past a partial blockage in the main.

Repeat clogs

The line was cleared recently and is slow again. Wall buildup is probably still there.

Sewer smell indoors

Odor near a basement drain can mean standing waste in a belly or a half-blocked main.

Lowest drain backs up first

Water finds the lowest opening. A floor drain or basement tub is usually the first to complain.

Winter Frost, Spring Storms and Your Main Line

Massachusetts frost line sits at 48 inches, and shifting soil in a hard winter can nudge old joints out of line. Spring thaw then sends roots after the extra moisture. Spring is a common time for a line that limped along all winter to finally stop.

Heavy rain adds a second stress. The Town prohibits sump pumps, downspouts and foundation drains from connecting to the sanitary sewer, because extra stormwater overloads the mains. If a backup happens only in a downpour, the cause may be shared capacity or an illegal connection rather than a clog we can jet away.

Keeping the Line Clear After We Leave

Jetting gives you a reset, not permanent protection. Grease keeps going down kitchen drains and roots keep growing. Wipes sold as flushable are a frequent culprit, and the Town of Danvers says they do not break down and they clog sewer pipes.
