Shoe Makers and a Reservoir: A Short Local Background

According to the town's open space plan, Putnamville and Danvers Highlands were noted for their early shoe manufacturing industry. The state historical survey of Danvers says Putnamville was earlier called Blind Hole, that the Putnam family ran the largest shoe firm in the village and that the town's earliest surviving school building went up there in 1852.

Houses and buildings of many ages can share a street in a village like this, so we assume nothing about the pipes behind your walls.

The Putnamville Reservoir lies in the north end of the town, and it supplies drinking water to Salem and Beverly. The Salem and Beverly Water Supply Board's 2024 water quality report lists it as a source.

Danvers runs its own system, and most of its water comes from Middleton Pond, treated at the Vernon C. Russell plant in Middleton. Living near the reservoir therefore does not mean your tap water comes from it. Your water bill shows who your supplier is.

Reading the Town's Water Test Results

In 2023 the town's lead tap sampling found a 90th percentile of 4.7 parts per billion, with 1 of 32 sampled homes above the 15 parts per billion action level. Copper came in at 0.0764 parts per million, with none of the 32 above the 1.3 limit. The town adjusts fluoride to about 0.7 parts per million.

Those results describe sampled homes across Danvers, not your tap. If you want to know about your own plumbing, ask the Danvers Water Division for its annual water quality report and consider testing a sample from your tap at a certified lab. A plumber can identify the material of the visible pipe where your water enters the house.

Who Owns Which Pipe: The Curb Stop Rule

The Town of Danvers owns, repairs and maintains the water service up to and including the curb stop. The owner is generally responsible from the curb stop to the building when the stop is in the public way, and for the service pipe on the owner's property. That is useful to know when you find a wet patch in the yard or a sudden jump in your water bill.

If the leak is between the curb box and your house, it is yours to fix. Water service line repair covers the pipe from the curb stop to the foundation, and leak detection locates the leak so the crew opens one spot instead of the whole lawn.

Toilets, Water Heaters and Everyday Repairs

A toilet that runs after flushing usually has a worn flapper or fill valve, and our toilet repair page explains the fix. A toilet that clogs repeatedly points to something deeper in the line. Please keep wipes out of the bowl, since the town says so-called flushable wipes do not break down.

For a tank that leaks or no longer heats, see water heater repair. Massachusetts law says that minor repairs such as fixing a leaky faucet or clearing a blocked drain need no permit, but replacing or relocating a faucet is not minor. Only a licensed plumber can pull a permit, and Danvers Inspectional Services can tell you how it applies to your job.

Frozen Pipes and Cold Weather

The Massachusetts residential building code lists a frost depth of 48 inches, which is why foundations and water service lines are set deep. Pipes in unheated garages, crawl spaces, exterior walls and attics are another matter, because they can freeze even when the buried line does not. In a hard freeze, open cabinet doors under sinks on exterior walls and let a thin stream of water run.

If a pipe has already split, shut off the main water valve and open a cold tap to drain the line. Then call. Our burst pipe repair page explains what a plumber does and why shutting off the water first matters.
