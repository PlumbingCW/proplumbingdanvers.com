A toilet that is about to overflow cannot wait. For toilet repair in Danvers, MA, our 24/7 line sends a licensed plumber any hour, and the steps in the card below buy you time: close the supply valve, lift the tank lid and stop the water.

Most toilet problems come down to four parts: the fill valve, the flapper, the wax ring and the trap. This page explains which is which, and when a repair makes more sense than a new toilet.

Stop an Overflow in Under a Minute

Do these in order when water is rising in the bowl.

  • 1Take the lid off the tank and push the flapper down into its seat to stop more water entering the bowl.
  • 2Reach behind the toilet and turn the oval or round supply valve clockwise until it stops.
  • 3If the valve will not turn, lift the float arm and tie it up with a coat hanger or a pencil.
  • 4Do not flush again, and put towels around the base until the plumber arrives.

Never pour drain chemicals into a toilet that is full, because the water will splash back on you.

Reading the Symptom Before Touching a Part

Water at the base

A damp ring around the bottom usually points to a failed wax ring or a loose closet bolt. Water that appears only after a flush fits that pattern.

Tank keeps refilling

A flapper that no longer seals lets tank water leak into the bowl, so the fill valve cycles on by itself. A dye tablet in the tank shows it within minutes.

Weak or slow flush

Mineral buildup in the rim holes or a partly blocked trap cuts the flush. A tank water level set too low does the same.

Rocking toilet

A toilet that shifts when you sit has lost its grip on the flange. Leave it alone and the wax seal breaks, which leads to hidden floor damage.

A Running Toilet Is Never Free

A running toilet wastes water around the clock, and on a metered home that shows up on the bill. The usual culprit is a worn flapper, a tired fill valve, or a chain that is too tight to let the flapper close.

Danvers sends tap water through a system that applies corrosion control, and the town report notes that white scale on fixtures may be calcium carbonate. That same scale can coat a flapper seat or clog the small fill valve inlet, so a rough, chalky seat is worth wiping before you buy a new part.

Two running toilets in one house often mean one cause upstream, such as high pressure. A pressure gauge on an outside spigot answers it in a minute.

Wax Rings, Flanges and Leaks at the Floor

The wax ring is a donut of sealant between the toilet outlet and the closet flange on the floor. When the seal fails, water or sewer gas gets out at the base. Replacing it means shutting off the water, draining the tank, lifting the toilet and setting it on a fresh ring.

The flange matters as much as the ring. In older Danvers houses, a cast iron or corroded flange can crack, and a flange sitting below the finished floor will not hold a seal. Roughly 43.5 percent of housing here was built before 1960, so we check the flange every time the toilet comes up. A repair ring or a new flange is far cheaper than rotted subfloor.

Clogs: Plunger, Auger, Then the Line

A single clogged toilet is usually a local clog, solved with a flange plunger or a closet auger that will not scratch the porcelain. If the tub and floor drain gurgle when you flush, the blockage is further down, and that is a drain cleaning job.

The Town of Danvers says that so-called flushable wipes do not break down and clog sewer pipes. That is true inside your own plumbing as well, so wipes belong in the trash. Repeated clogs after a toilet stopped working well can also mean roots in the line, in which case a camera tells the story, see sewer inspection.

What Drives the Price

A flapper swap and a new toilet are very different jobs. You hear the price before anything is taken apart.
