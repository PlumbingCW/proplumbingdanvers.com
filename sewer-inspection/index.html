Sewer Camera Inspection in Danvers, MA

A sewer camera inspection in Danvers, MA puts a small waterproof camera on a flexible rod and sends it down your drain line, so you see roots, cracks, sags and blockages on a screen instead of guessing. We record the pass, mark the spot and explain what it means.

People call before buying a house, after repeated clogs or ahead of a landscaping project. The inspection is information first, and repair only comes up if the footage shows a real defect.

What the Camera Actually Shows

The camera head carries its own lights and sends live video to a monitor. A locator transmitter in the head lets us trace the pipe above ground, so we can say how deep it runs and where it crosses the yard. We pull the camera back slowly and note every joint.

According to the Town of Danvers, its Sewer Division maintains over 125 miles of gravity sewer main, and a camera shows whether a problem sits on your side of the connection.

What shows well: roots entering at joints, hairline and longitudinal cracks, offset or separated joints, sagging stretches where water pools, scale in cast iron and grease coating. These are the things that cause repeat backups.

Buying a Home: Why a Recording Matters

A home inspector looks at the house, not the pipe buried under the lawn. Because 25.4 percent of Danvers housing dates from 1939 or earlier and about 69.5 percent from before 1980, an old lateral is a real possibility on many streets. A recording gives you a bargaining fact, not a hunch.

Footage lets you show a seller, an engineer or another contractor the same picture. It also gives you a baseline, so if the line backs up after you move in, you can compare. Ask for the file and keep it with your closing papers.

If the camera finds trouble, see sewer line repair for how fixes differ. If it finds only buildup, a plan from hydro jetting may be enough.

Roots, Sags and the Slow Backup

Roots look for water and small leaks, and a joint with a hairline gap is an invitation. They grow thicker over seasons, trapping paper and grease until the pipe closes. A single camera pass shows whether you have a few threads or a full mat.

A sag, sometimes called a belly, holds water in a low spot. Waste settles there and the next flush stalls. Cleaning helps for a while, but only a repair restores the slope, which the camera shows by standing water in the footage.

If the line is full of debris, we may need to clear it before the camera can see the pipe. In that case a short cleaning comes first, which our sewer line cleaning page describes.

Honest Limits of a Camera Inspection

A camera sees only the pipe it reaches. A sharp bend, a collapsed section or a full blockage can stop it, and then we report what we saw up to that point. It cannot look through soil, so it will not tell you what is outside the pipe.

It also does not decide ownership. The Town of Danvers owns and repairs the main line sewer, so if the defect sits in the town main rather than your pipe, the Sewer Division is the right call. Ask where responsibility ends at your address.

Seasons, Storms and the Right Time to Look

A line often behaves best in dry weather and worst after a long rain. A camera pass is still useful in storm season, but high groundwater can mask small leaks and make the pipe harder to read. If your concern is a backup in storms, tell us so we can watch for infiltration at joints.

The Town prohibits sump pumps, roof downspouts and exterior foundation drains from connecting to the building sewer. If the camera sees clear groundwater flowing in, or you have a sump discharging inside, we flag it. Our sump pump page covers alternatives.
