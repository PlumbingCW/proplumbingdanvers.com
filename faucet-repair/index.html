Water Is Spraying: Do This First

Stop the Water at the Fixture

Every sink, toilet and appliance should have its own shutoff valve.

  • 1Look under the sink for two small valves, one hot and one cold, and turn each clockwise until snug.
  • 2If a valve is frozen or leaks when turned, close the main shutoff where the line enters the house.
  • 3Open the faucet to drain the lines and relieve pressure, then wipe the cabinet dry.
  • 4Keep towels and a bucket in place, and call the 24/7 line if the leak continues.

Do not force a stuck valve with pliers. A cracked stem turns a drip into a flood.

Start With the Faucet Type

How a faucet is repaired depends on how it is built inside. Four designs cover nearly every home, and each wears out in its own way. Knowing yours saves a trip for parts.

According to the Danvers Water Division's annual water quality report, white scaling on faucets and shower heads may come from calcium carbonate, so not every crusty fixture is a failed one.

Why Fixtures Here Show Wear Early

The Danvers water report mentions white scale on faucets and shower heads that may come from calcium carbonate. Scale grinds on cartridges and clogs aerators, so a low-flow faucet or one that sputters is often just a clogged screen. Unscrew the aerator, soak it in vinegar and look at the screen before you call.

Older homes bring their own issues. About 43.5 percent of Danvers housing was built before 1960, and fixtures from that era often have corroded shutoffs that never get used. If a valve has not moved in years, expect it to weep when we turn it, and we plan for that.

Supply Lines and Shutoff Valves

Braided supply lines run between the shutoff valve and the faucet. They fail without warning when the crimped end corrodes or the mesh frays, and a burst line can empty into a cabinet for hours. If yours is more than a decade old, replace it as a matter of routine.

The shutoff valve itself is another weak point. Old multi-turn valves often drip from the stem or refuse to seal. Quarter-turn ball valves are the replacement we favor because they are easy to operate in an emergency and have fewer parts to wear.

Shower and Tub Troubles

Showerhead drips after use

A worn cartridge or seat in the shower valve is the usual cause. Replacing it normally means opening a small access plate.

Water too hot or cold

A pressure balancing cartridge that has scaled up may no longer mix well. It is a safety issue because of scalding.

Tub spout leaks

The diverter on a tub spout fails first. A few drops from the spout while the shower runs means water is leaking past it.

Stain on the wall or ceiling below

A leak behind the tile may be a failed valve body or a pipe joint. Do not ignore a damp wall.

A drain that backs up with the tub is not a fixture fault. See drain cleaning for that, and if water appears where no fixture exists, a trace under leak detection tells you more.

Repair, Replace or Relocate

A faucet repair is usually small, but a replacement is a different category. Massachusetts treats minor work like fixing a leaky faucet as not needing a permit, while replacing or relocating a faucet is not minor. State rules say no plumbing is installed or replaced until the local inspector has issued a permit, and permits go only to licensed plumbers.

So a new kitchen faucet, a moved sink or a swapped shower valve is a job for a licensed plumber. Danvers Inspectional Services at 1 Sylvan Street handles local permits. You can check any plumber's license through the state license lookup on mass.gov.
