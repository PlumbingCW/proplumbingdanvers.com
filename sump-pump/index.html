Pump Quit During a Storm: Triage Before We Arrive

While water is rising in the basement

Safety comes before the pump. Water and electricity together are the real hazard in a flooded basement.

  • ✓Do not step into standing water if the basement outlets or panel are low and wet.
  • ✓Check that the pump is plugged in and that the GFCI outlet or breaker has not tripped.
  • ✓Look at the discharge pipe outside and clear any ice, leaves or a crushed section.
  • ✓Lift the float by hand. If the pump starts, the float switch is the likely failure.
  • ✓Move valuables and stored boxes onto shelves or bins above the floor.

If the pump hums but moves no water, the impeller may be jammed or the check valve stuck. If it makes no sound at all, the motor or power supply has failed. Tell the dispatcher which one you hear, since it changes what the plumber loads onto the truck.

Why Danvers Basements Get Wet

The town has a documented flooding history. In the Mother's Day Flood of May 2006, the four-foot barriers at the Danvers Electric station were breached. The town reports flooding near Town Center and along the Porter River, Beaver Brook, Crane River and Waters River, and its 2019 Hazard Mitigation Plan rates flooding high frequency and serious severity. A basement near those waterways deserves a working pump and a backup.

Beaver Brook and the Crane River area sit in a FEMA zone AE flood hazard area. If your property is near either, ask the town whether it is mapped, because a high water table means the pump runs often and wears out sooner. The same is true across the line in Middleton, where Ipswich River flooding stresses sump pumps and septic fields together.

A Rule Every Danvers Owner Should Know

The Town prohibits connecting sump pumps to the town sewer, with no grandfathering. Roof downspouts, exterior foundation drains and area drains may not connect to the building sewer either, under Sewer Regulations Section III. Stormwater and groundwater do not belong in the sanitary sewer.

The practical effect: the pump must discharge outdoors, away from the foundation, and not into a floor drain, a laundry sink or a pipe that ties to the sewer. If a past owner piped it that way, a plumber can reroute it. This also protects you, because a flooded sewer system can push water back into a basement with a connection like that.

Most pumps wear out gradually, not suddenly, and a unit that has run through many spring seasons is a candidate for replacement even if it still works. A failed pump on the first night of a nor'easter is the expensive way to learn that.

Floats, Backup Power and Alarms

The float switch is the pump's brain, and it is the part that fails most often. A tethered float can snag on the pit wall, a vertical float can stick against a pipe, and either one leaves a healthy motor idle. A pit that is too narrow makes this worse, which is why we check the pit size and the float travel on every visit.

Storms often cut power, which is exactly when the pump is needed. A battery backup system adds a second pump, a deep cycle battery and a charger that takes over when the main pump or the electricity fails. A water level alarm gives you warning before the pit overflows. Together they turn a bad night into a manageable one.

Installing a Sump Pump in an Indoor Pit

A new installation starts with the pit. A plumber cuts the slab, digs a basin, sets a liner, installs the pump on a stable base, and runs a discharge line with a check valve to the exterior. Cutting a floor makes dust and noise, and a licensed plumber pulls any permit that the work needs.

Locate the low point

We look for where water enters and where the slab slopes, since the pit goes where water naturally gathers.

Cut and dig

The slab is saw cut, the soil removed and a basin set level so the float swings free.

Set the pump and discharge

The pump sits on a firm base, a check valve goes on the discharge, and the pipe is routed outdoors, never to the sewer.

Test under load

We fill the pit, watch the float cycle, check the check valve and confirm the discharge point is clear.

A pump that runs nonstop after a dry week can also point to a hidden supply leak, which leak detection can rule in or out. If your basement has damp walls or seepage that a pump alone cannot solve, see our page on basement waterproofing. For flooding that comes up through floor drains, the issue is the sewer, and a sewer line repair inspection is the next step.

Keeping the Pump Ready Year Round

  • Pour a bucket of water into the pit each spring and watch the pump start and stop.
  • Clean debris from the pit and the pump intake once a year.
  • Test the battery backup twice a year and replace the battery on schedule.
  • Check the outdoor discharge end so it points away from the foundation and is not buried or frozen.
