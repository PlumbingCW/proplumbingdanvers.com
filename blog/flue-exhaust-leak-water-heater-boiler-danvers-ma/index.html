What A Flue Is Supposed To Do

A gas water heater or boiler burns fuel and produces carbon dioxide, water vapor and trace carbon monoxide. The flue, a sealed metal pipe or masonry chimney liner, carries those gases up and outside on warm rising air. If the flue is cracked, disconnected or blocked, the gases spill into the room instead.

Backdrafting is the term for the reverse. Air pressure in a tight house, a clogged chimney or a failed vent fan can pull exhaust back down the flue and out through the draft hood. The result is incomplete combustion and carbon monoxide in your living space.

According to Massachusetts standards, only a plumber licensed by the Board of State Examiners of Plumbers and Gas Fitters may do this kind of work on a heater or boiler.

Six Signs Worth Taking Seriously

Soot or scorch marks

Black streaking above the draft hood, on the burner door or around the vent collar means flame or hot gas is escaping where it should not.

Rust streaks on the vent

Orange stains on a galvanized connector, or white crust at a joint, point to condensation leaking from a seam.

A yellow, floppy flame

A healthy burner flame is mostly blue. Lazy yellow flames suggest poor combustion or poor draft.

Stuffy air, headaches

If symptoms ease when you leave the house, treat it as a possible CO exposure and call 911.

Moisture on the windows

Excess indoor humidity near the appliance can mean combustion moisture is staying in the room.

Chronic alarm chirps

A CO alarm that trips again and again is telling you something. Do not remove the battery.

Checks A Homeowner Can Do Safely

Look at the vent pipe from a distance with a flashlight. Look for sagging sections, gaps at joints, a loose collar at the top of the heater and rust stains at the elbows. Check that nothing is stored against or near the unit and the room has air to burn.

Do not tape, caulk or foil-wrap a flue. Never relight a pilot that has gone out repeatedly. Never hold a flame near a vent to test draft, because leaking gas is also possible.

What An Inspection Looks At

A technician starts with the vent path: draft hood, connector, any horizontal runs and the termination at the roof or wall. Slope matters, since connectors should rise toward the chimney so hot gases keep moving upward.

Then comes a combustion check with an analyzer that reads carbon monoxide at the flue. The same visit looks at the burner, the heat exchanger on a boiler and the combustion air supply. Findings are explained before any repair begins, with the price agreed first.

Who Repairs What

A licensed plumber and gasfitter can inspect the vent, reseat or replace connectors and check combustion. If the heater is the source, our water heater repair service handles gas models, and a failed boiler flue is a job for boiler repair. A sealed combustion boiler needs different vent parts than an atmospheric one, so the model matters.

A chimney liner or masonry repair often comes from a chimney professional, not a plumber. We will tell you where our work stops. We dispatch to Beverly and Middleton as well, so a neighbor with the same symptoms can call the same line.

Keeping The Vent Healthy After A Repair

Once a vent has been repaired, a few habits keep it that way. Keep the area around the heater or boiler clear, since stored paint, cleaners and cardboard do not belong beside a burner. Leave the combustion air openings unblocked, and never close off a vent grille to save heat.

Test your carbon monoxide alarms on the schedule printed on the unit, and replace them when the manufacturer says so. An alarm near the sleeping areas and one on each floor give the earliest warning. Remember that the alarm is a backup, not a substitute for an inspection.

Watch for the old signs returning. New soot, fresh rust streaks, a pilot that keeps going out or a burner flame that turns yellow all mean the problem was not fully solved or has come back. Shut the appliance off and call a licensed plumber. Annual service keeps small vent problems from becoming urgent ones.
